# The Automation Ownership Act — Consolidated Framework

## Core Thesis

Automation will not merely displace blue-collar labor — it will appropriate, without compensation, the value workers generate through their daily labor in the form of training data for the very machines that replace them. Rather than addressing this through income redistribution (UBI, robot taxes), this framework establishes a legal, non-transferable **ownership stake** for workers in the automation that displaces them — transforming displacement from a condition of dependency into a source of ongoing income and structural leverage.

**The foundational principle:** workers own the data provenance of the machines that replace them. Robots are trained on the real-time corrections workers provide on the production line, and workers therefore hold a legal claim on the resulting hardware — not merely a wage for their labor.

---

## The Hardware Ownership Mechanism

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Digital Ticket** | A non-tradeable, body-tied credential earned through required "Coach" hours working alongside sensors and robots on the actual line — this is how the AI model receives real-world correction data. |
| **Loan Activation** | Once activated, the ticket unlocks a **zero-interest government loan** (administered via smart contract, not discretionary state control) to purchase a robot. |
| **Outright Purchase Ban** | Operating companies (factories, logistics firms — any entity deploying robots) are **permanently prohibited from purchasing robots outright, from any source, at any time** — including from retiring workers. They must **rent from workers**, with no exception. The only entity permitted to buy back hardware is the AI/robotics manufacturer, not the operating company that was renting it. |
| **Dual Income Streams** | Workers receive **Wage A** (data/sampling compensation) and **Wage B** (robot rental income). |
| **Skill Progression** | Mastery unlocks tickets for more advanced robots; basic tickets are guaranteed to every worker in the trade. |
| **Upgrades** | Hardware upgrades occur through trade-in value deducted from a rolling loan as technology advances. |
| **Retirement Pension** | Calculated from the market value of past robot assets and skill tier — not a discretionary handout. |

---

## Administrative Layer

- A shared smart-contract administrative layer handles loan origination, dissolution triggers, and enforcement.
- **Anti-circumvention clause:** any entity supplying control software, model weights, or the perception stack for a rented robot is jointly bound by this Act, regardless of corporate structure — this closes the loophole whereby valuable IP is spun into a nominally separate, unregulated entity that licenses "dumb" hardware to a thin operating shell.

---

## Sunset and the "Long-Tail Corrector" Problem

**Open paradox identified:** if AI never achieves full self-iteration, human correction data is needed indefinitely — yet an age-based retirement trigger (tickets dissolve when a worker retires) cuts off that data supply regardless of whether the system still requires it.

**Resolution:** replace the age-based sunset with a **technical threshold trigger** — dissolution occurs only when new human-corrected data ceases to measurably improve robot performance for that trade. Until then, a shrinking, more specialized pool of certified "long-tail correctors" remains employed and compensated, with *increasing* per-person leverage as their numbers decline (scarcity works in their favor). This mirrors how even advanced automated systems still rely on human feedback for edge cases today — it serves as a hedge against genuine uncertainty about whether AI will ever stop needing human input, rather than a confident bet either way.

> **Note:** this system-level trigger governs whether a *trade* still requires correctors. An individual worker's retirement is a separate, personal event — addressed below.

## Retirement Asset Disposition

At individual retirement, a worker must choose one of two structurally defined paths for their robot:

### Path 1: Donate to Public Utility
The robot enters an open-source public automation pool, consistent with the commons-dissolution design already built into the shared administrative layer and the exit ramp for chronically unmatched robots.

### Path 2: Sell Back to the AI/Robotics Manufacturer
- Not to the operating company that was renting it.
- The manufacturer buys the hardware back for cash and either reclaims it for raw materials, or reintroduces it into circulation through the same ticket-issuance pipeline as new equipment.
- A manufacturer buyback can **never** result in the hardware becoming directly owned or operated by a factory or logistics firm — that would recreate the outright-purchase loophole the rent-only rule exists to prevent.

**Pension sequencing:** pension disbursement should be **structurally sequenced with this liquidation** — the realized value of whichever path is chosen feeds directly into the pension calculation (market value of past robot assets + skill tier), rather than being computed as a separate, independent government payment.

---

### Three Design Risks

| Risk | Description | Mitigation |
|------|-------------|------------|
| **1. Incentive Imbalance** | If manufacturer buyback consistently yields a larger pension than public donation, the public automation pool will be starved of assets. | Both paths should draw from the same valuation floor (tied to the depreciation/trade-in schedule) so the choice reflects genuine preference, not a payout gap. |
| **2. Bargaining Power** | A retiring worker negotiating buyback price with the manufacturer is structurally weak — they need cash, and the manufacturer has incentive to lowball. | A floor price indexed to the depreciation schedule, rather than open bilateral negotiation. |
| **3. Demand-Side Concentration** | If most retiring workers in a trade choose corporate buyback, a single firm could accumulate a large hardware share through ordinary retirement cycles. | The companion market-share cap should explicitly cover this, not just bankruptcy-driven or day-to-day rental concentration. |

## Political and Coalition Strategy

- **"Forbidden Fruit" framing:** AI/robotics companies lose the right to sell robots outright, but gain clean, provenance-tracked training data, guaranteed funding via the loan structure, and political cover as allies of labor rather than displacers of it. This trade should be stated plainly in the bill's preamble.

- **Scope the bill to brick-and-mortar/physical enterprises only.** This is the strongest political move identified: it removes software-only and non-physical firms from the opposition coalition entirely. Precedent: right-to-repair legislation has advanced further industry-by-industry than as an omnibus law.

- **Coalition composition:** workers + AI/robotics firms + government form a workable alliance, but **physical enterprises (factory owners, logistics, manufacturers) are a necessary fourth party** who bear a pure cost increase under this bill. Their buy-in — not just worker/AI-firm agreement — is the actual bottleneck.

- **Banks and administrative capacity** are independent variables. Political agreement doesn't automatically produce a working credit system, skill-tier certification bodies, or dispute resolution — these require real institution-building regardless of consensus.

---

## Capital Flight and the "Why Stay" Question

> **The relevant comparison** is never "advanced automation vs. obsolete machinery" — it's the *same* robot, owned free-and-clear elsewhere vs. rentable-only under this law domestically. A firm relocating to avoid Wage B is pursuing lower total cost of ownership for identical technology, which is the ordinary logic of capital mobility.

The bill's survival depends on **quantifying**, not just asserting, that licensed/provenance-clean data is worth enough to firms to offset the cost of Wage B — this is currently the weakest, least-evidenced claim in the proposal.

A global standardized framework ("other nations must follow or face economic shock") is a categorically larger and slower project than the domestic bill — multilateral coordination problems typically take decades. This should be framed as a separate, longer-horizon aspiration.

## Risk Allocation: Bankruptcy and Idle Robots

**Problem:** the worker owns the robot and the government loan, not the company. If the renting company fails, Wage B rental income stops — but the worker's loan obligation doesn't. Without a fix, workers absorb the downside risk of company failure while companies absorb none of it.

**Resolution — reframe idle robots as a matching problem, not an asset-loss problem:**

- Because ownership sits with the worker, not the company, bankruptcy releases the robot back onto the market as **pre-financed, ready-to-deploy capital** — a genuine low-barrier entry point for new entrepreneurs.
- Requires two supporting mechanisms:

  1. A **matching/exchange layer** within the smart-contract admin system so idle robots surface to new renters quickly.
  2. A **bridge fund** (cross-subsidized by active Wage B streams, similar in spirit to unemployment insurance) covering workers' loan payments during the gap between a company's failure and a new renter being matched.

---

### Fair-Matching Algorithm

Rentals should be allocated using a **continuous priority rank** (e.g., by cumulative rental income or idle days to date, lowest first) rather than a binary "never rented / already rented" tier.

| Principle | Implementation |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Two-Pass System** | Filter for task-compatible robots first, then apply fairness-priority ranking within that eligible pool — otherwise the algorithm forces bad matches and renters route around the system informally. |
| **Exit Ramp** | Chronically unmatched robots (idle through multiple fairness cycles) need resolution — early buy-back, retraining credit toward a new ticket — rather than indefinite queue priority, or they become debt against a permanently unrentable asset. |

---

## Cross-Trade Valuation Gap

**Problem identified:** the bill's equity mechanisms only address valuation gaps *within* a trade — skill mastery unlocks access to higher-tier robots, and the fair-matching algorithm equalizes *access* to rental opportunities for a given robot type. Neither touches the much larger gap *between* trades: a robot performing high-margin precision work is worth dramatically more than a robot performing low-margin repetitive work. Two equally expert "Coaches" in different trades can end up with very different Wage B income and loan payoff timelines for reasons entirely outside their control or effort.

**Resolution — cross-trade equalization pool.** This mirrors what sovereign wealth funds and resource dividends solve when the underlying resource isn't evenly distributed: revenue is pooled and distributed per capita *before* being split by which specific well someone happens to work near.

> **Implementation:** a mandatory, marginal cross-subsidy where higher-value-robot trades contribute a small share of surplus Wage B into a shared fund that tops up lower-value-robot trades — so the guaranteed floor becomes "basic robot, valuation-adjusted, system-wide" rather than just "basic robot within your own trade."

---

### Hard Tradeoff

| Too Much Equalization | Too Little Equalization |
|-----------------------|-------------------------|
| Erodes the incentive to master a high-value trade | Leaves cross-trade inequality fully intact from day one, independent of anyone's effort |

The equalization rate (what share of surplus Wage B gets pooled, and how it's redistributed) must be calibrated carefully enough to preserve the skill-mastery incentive — this is a design parameter still to be worked out.

## Ownership Caps and Concentration Limits

**Requirement:** strict caps on individual ownership are necessary to prevent asset concentration and capital monopolization.

- The non-tradeable, body-tied nature of the Digital Ticket implies a natural cap — one ticket, held by one worker, cannot be transferred or pooled. **This should be made explicit as a hard rule:** a limited number of active robots per worker at a time, with skill-tier mastery determining *which value/type* of robot a worker can hold, not additional units.

- **Enforcement risk** mirrors the taxi-medallion/fishing-quota problem: a hard per-worker cap doesn't stop informal circumvention — a worker could nominally hold the ticket while a hidden capital interest operates the robot through a power-of-attorney or management arrangement. The anti-circumvention clause should extend to any arrangement where ticket-holding is functionally decoupled from actual "Coach" participation and asset control.

- **This addresses supply-side concentration, not demand-side concentration.** Capping how many robots one worker can own doesn't prevent a small number of large rental companies from aggregating de facto market power by renting from thousands of dispersed worker-owners simultaneously. A companion limit (e.g., an antitrust-style cap on what share of a regional robot supply any single renting company can source from) may be needed.

## Viability Compared to Mainstream Policy

Mainstream approaches to AI/automation labor impact cluster around:

| Approach | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| Redistribution | UBI, robot tax |
| Labor-market cushioning | Portable benefits, retraining |
| Governance/voice | Co-determination, sectoral bargaining |
| Data dividends | Mostly symbolic today |

This proposal is structurally different — it targets **asset ownership**, not just income flow, closer in kind to land reform or fishing-quota allocation than to UBI.

| Strength vs. Mainstream | Biggest Viability Risk |
|-------------------------|------------------------|
| Directly answers the "dependency" critique of UBI (a ticket-holder is an owner, not a recipient). | Every historical precedent for non-tradeable, individually-held rights (taxi medallions, fishing quotas, land-reform plots) has eventually been informally circumvented under enough financial pressure — this bill would face that pressure at a much larger scale. |

> **Bottom line:** stronger as agenda-shifting political writing that names a real gap in current thinking than as a bill likely to pass and be enforced in its current form in the near term. Worth treating as two different documents with two different jobs.
